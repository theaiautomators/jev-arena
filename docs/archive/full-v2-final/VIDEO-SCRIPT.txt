Before you replace anything, though, there are three things to understand: what these models do, what we actually tested, and where a leaderboard can mislead you.

Imagine a support message arrives about a duplicate charge. Your system needs to choose Billing, Engineering, Customer Success or Review. It might also need a yes-or-no urgency decision, or a score on a defined scale.

A generative language model can write those answers. A decision model focuses on choosing among outcomes you supply, often returning scores or probabilities directly. That can make it useful inside a larger workflow. It can still be confidently wrong.

Jev is TypeSafe's hosted service. This run uses a pinned version, Jev one point thirteen. TypeSafe is the company behind Jev, not another competitor in this table.

Laya is the small local option. Its main English checkpoint is around four hundred and twenty-one million parameters. I also tested its typed-decision and multilingual versions as separate entries. Those related checkpoints have different intended uses; their results aren't interchangeable.

Plumb and Decider are four-billion-parameter decision models. Decider here is the pinned version-two checkpoint, not a claim about every later release.

Winnow is a twelve-billion-parameter model running through its own runtime with eight-bit quantized weights. Nimble uses a decision adapter with a nine-billion-parameter backbone.

SemIf takes another approach: it reads candidate-answer scores from a base model without adding a new decision fine-tune. That lets us compare it with the same Qwen backbone generating a JSON answer. Our Qwen baseline uses greedy decoding, thinking disabled and a small response budget. We didn't test every possible Qwen prompt or decoding setup.

CLM compares representations of the state and possible actions using a learned scoring head. ModernBERT provides an entailment-based classification control. And the uniform control assigns equal probability to every option, then picks the first label when tied. That's deterministic, not random guessing.

Thirteen entries therefore means several approaches, related checkpoints and controls. It doesn't mean thirteen independent architectures.

For the main run, each entry received seven thousand, six hundred and seventy-one static cases. Across the roster, that's just under a hundred thousand saved records, including failed and unsupported attempts.

Two thousand cases per model use another model's target. I keep teacher agreement separate. Agreeing with a teacher doesn't prove an answer is correct.

The remaining five thousand, six hundred and seventy-one reference cases combine public decision questions, classification, multilingual data, evidence relevance and generated policy fixtures with executable rules. Related variations share templates, so they aren't all independent real-world scenarios. Public training exposure is unknown.

Five answers apart. That's a sensitivity check, not a new winning score.

The second trap is mixing decision quality with output formatting.

Arena's strict score requires the correct label and a valid output. We chose a fixed tolerance for probabilities adding up to one. Some outputs choose the right label but fail that numerical rule.

Across all five thousand, six hundred and seventy-one references, Jev scores about ninety-two percent strict, or ninety-two point eight when we count selected labels regardless of probability formatting.

Decider scores eighty-three point eight percent strict, but ninety-two point three on selected labels. That's only twenty-eight labels behind Jev. Describing that whole strict-score gap as bad reasoning would be wrong.

Nimble scores ninety point three percent under both measures and has the strongest local strict result on the full reference denominator.

On the shared cohort, Jev's lead over Winnow is twenty-nine labels. The descriptive uncertainty interval for Decider versus Jev includes either side ahead. That doesn't establish equivalence. These are results on this suite, with shared templates and unadjusted comparisons.

Speed matters too. Laya's serial medians are around the mid-teens of milliseconds in the repeated blocks. That's fast, but its main English profile matches about sixty-five percent of labels on the shared cohort, versus Jev's ninety-five percent. Its other checkpoints were tested across the full suite too, and neither closes that gap here.

SemIf's shared label agreement is about eighty-two percent, compared with roughly eighty-six for our Qwen JSON baseline. Its serial requests are much faster, but that isn't equal quality at a guaranteed speedup.

These are deployment measurements. Sizes, precision and software differ. Hosted timing includes network travel. We used one request at a time, so this doesn't measure maximum server throughput.

CLM performed poorly in this profile. Before the run, we checked its native path on thirty-six short development probes. A batch-invariant runtime mode brought probabilities into agreement, but changed its speed. That limited parity check does not prove every long input is correct, or that the architecture can never work well.

We took two hundred Choice cases and tested Jev plus the three leading eligible local contenders again. Then we reversed the options while preserving what each option meant.

For exact repeats, Decider and Nimble kept all two hundred labels. Winnow kept all one hundred and eighty-seven labels it could return; thirteen requests hit that banking option limit. Jev kept one hundred and ninety-nine of two hundred.

The reversed-order runs also differed from the original run. Nimble changed eleven out of two hundred. Decider changed four. Winnow changed four out of one hundred and eighty-seven comparable answers. Some changed answers improved, some got worse. Those differences can reflect option order or run-to-run variation; this check doesn't isolate the cause. Stable labels also don't guarantee valid probability vectors. The dashboard evidence keeps those counts separate.
